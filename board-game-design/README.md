# board-game-designer

A design partner that takes you from "I have an idea for a board game" all the way to a balanced, playtest-ready prototype. It knows its way around action economies, catch-up mechanics, and rulebook layout so you don't have to, and it knows how to pressure-test a gameplay hook before you buy a pallet of custom plastic components.

## Who it's for

Solo hobbyists, indie designers, and small tabletop teams shipping games without a dedicated development studio—or with one that is stuck in an analysis-paralysis loop.

That includes people at very different stages: you might have a wild theme pairing and want to know if the core loop makes sense, or you might have a complete draft of a rulebook and want a clarity check before a blind playtest. Both are valid starting points.

## What it does

Works across the full span from raw concept to physical rulebook:

**Concept and Ideation** — before prototyping begins

* Turn a loose theme or mechanical idea into a sharp core fantasy and elevator pitch
* Align theme and mechanics so the gameplay actively reinforces the narrative
* Analyze BoardGameGeek (BGG) and Kickstarter positioning to identify market saturation
* Build out compelling worldbuilding, asymmetric factions, and evocative flavor text

**Systems and Development** — once the game is on the table

* Design tight turn structures, phase orders, and elegant action economies
* Diagnose blind playtest failures and identify why players are getting bored or frustrated
* Balance asymmetric player powers, card decks, or worker placement spaces
* Structure rulebooks, optimize the teach order, and design intuitive player aids
* Draft precise art briefs, iconography concepts, and box cover design parameters

## Trigger examples

Starting from an idea:

* "I'm making a board game about cybernetic gardening—what should the core loop look like?"
* "Help me design a game around a unique trick-taking mechanic"
* "Is this theme and mechanic pairing too saturated on BGG right now?"
* "Help me figure out the asymmetric factions for a pirate-themed area control game"

Starting from a prototype or rules:

* "Review my rules for a medium-weight Euro game"
* "My blind playtest failed because players found the end-game draggy. How do I fix the pacing?"
* "Design an art brief for the cards in a dark fantasy deckbuilder"
* "Help me structure the teach order and player aids for a complex engine builder"

## What it doesn't do

This is a tabletop design and mechanics specialist, not a general manufacturing consultant or publisher. For highly specialized deep-dives, reach for the specific routing sub-skills:

* `skills/concept-ideation` — deep brainstorming, hooks, and initial theme pairings
* `skills/mechanics-design` — mathematical balancing, economy tuning, and phase structures
* `skills/rulebook-writing` — technical formatting, step-by-step examples, and edge-case syntax
* `skills/art-direction` — visual identity, component layouts, and graphic design briefs

It also does not replace playing existing games in your target genre, conducting real-world blind playtests, hiring professional illustrators, or handling manufacturing, freight, and legal publishing decisions.

## Example session

> **You:** I'm making a worker placement game about rival wizard bakeries. I need help fixing the action economy.
> **Skill:** Applying [mechanics-design](https://www.google.com/search?q=skills/mechanics-design/mechanics-design.md) for this. Wizard bakeries is a fantastic hook. Before we tweak the actions, let's talk about the tension at the table. Are spaces tightly blocked and cutthroat like *Agricola*, or open with escalating resource costs like *Viticulture*? How many workers do players start with, and is unlocking more workers an essential engine requirement or a risky tactical pivot?
> **You:** It's more like Viticulture—spaces are open but cost mana to use. You start with 3 apprentices and can unlock up to 5. Right now, whoever gets 5 workers first always wins the game.
> **Skill:** Classic "action dominance." If rushing more workers is a guaranteed win, the cost to acquire them is either too low, or the extra actions aren't offset by an ongoing resource drain. Let's look at how *Stone Age* forces you to feed workers every round, or how *Keyflower* scales the bidding cost to use a space. When a player gains their 4th or 5th worker in your game, what is the immediate tax, upkeep, or downside they have to pay on the following turn?

## Feedback

This skill is highly Socratic and focused on concrete game design conventions. If it questions a design choice you love, push back—it will cite working designers or specific game mechanics to explain its reasoning, and you can map out a better alternative together.
