# Theme/Mechanic Pairing

Use this when a designer has a theme without a mechanic, a mechanic without a theme, or a pairing that feels arbitrary.

## The integration test

A theme and a mechanic are well-paired when **changing one breaks the other.** If you could re-skin the game to a different theme without rewriting any rules, the theme is decoration, not design.

Examples of tight pairings:

- **Pandemic** — cooperative deck/draw because disease spreads while you're not looking. The "outbreak" mechanic only makes sense as disease; "outbreak of mail delivery" doesn't read.
- **Wingspan** — engine-building because birdwatching is collecting and observing patterns over time. Re-skinned to factories, the cards stop telling a story.
- **Spirit Island** — area control with cooperative scaling because the players *are* the land defending itself. The asymmetry is the spirits being different *kinds* of nature.
- **Brass: Birmingham** — network-building with a development track because that *is* what the Industrial Revolution was. The eras rule (canals → rails) only works because the history dictated it.

Examples of weak pairings (theme grafted on):

- A cube-pushing economic game re-skinned to "trading dragons." The dragons add nothing — replace them with cargo and the game is identical.
- A trick-taking game with a fantasy quest theme. Trick-taking is about reading hands and timing trumps; quests are about journey and stakes. They don't reinforce each other.

## Pairing prompts

When a designer brings a theme:

- What does the **player physically do** that mirrors what they'd do in the fiction? (Draft → choose, place → claim, push your luck → risk, hidden info → suspect)
- What is the **basic unit of currency** in the fiction? (Knowledge, food, faith, blood, time, attention)
- What does the fiction make **scarce**? Scarcity drives mechanics — if nothing is scarce in the fiction, the theme will struggle.
- What gets **lost or destroyed** in the fiction? Loss is one of the strongest dramatic engines mechanics can lean on.
- Who is the **opponent** in the fiction — other players, the world, the clock, yourself?

When a designer brings a mechanic:

- What human experience does this mechanic *feel like*? (Drafting feels like choosing a path; auctioning feels like wanting something more than you can afford; trick-taking feels like reading a room.)
- What kind of person *does this for a living* in the real world or in fiction? Start there for theme.
- What real moment of tension does this mechanic produce, and what fictional moment matches that tension?

## Bridging exercises

If the pairing is weak, try one of these:

1. **Strip and re-theme.** Describe the game with no theme at all — just the verbs and resources. Does any theme feel obvious? If three feel equally good, none is doing real work.
2. **Promote a mechanic to a theme.** Sometimes the mechanic *is* the theme. ("This game is about drafting" → it's a game about choosing under pressure → set it somewhere choosing under pressure is the whole drama: a draft night, a courtroom, a famine.)
3. **Promote a theme to a mechanic.** ("This game is about loneliness" → what mechanic makes loneliness felt? Solo turns with no shared resources? Information that no one else can see?)
4. **Find the contradiction.** Strong themes contain a contradiction (heroism + futility, ambition + corruption, love + betrayal). What contradiction does the theme contain, and what mechanic forces the player to live in it?

## Signs the pairing is working

- The designer can describe a single **table moment** that only makes sense with this theme and this mechanic together.
- A non-designer playtester says something like "oh, that's just like when…" — the mechanic evoked the fiction unprompted.
- The rulebook examples write themselves — the rules and the fiction explain each other.
