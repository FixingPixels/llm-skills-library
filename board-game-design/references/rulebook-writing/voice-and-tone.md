# Voice and Tone in Rulebooks

The rulebook has multiple voices, and they're not the same.

## The three voices

1. **Procedural voice** — for actual rules. Crisp, second person, present tense, imperative. "Draw a card. Place it in your row. Score 1 point."
2. **In-world voice** — for the introduction, flavor headers, and any framing that's about the world. Whatever your game's voice is — formal, mythic, sardonic, intimate, hopeful.
3. **Designer voice** — for designer notes at the back. Personal, reflective, warm. *Different* from the rulebook proper — this is where you talk to the player about why you made the game.

Don't mix these. A rule that breaks into in-world voice mid-sentence ("The villagers, weary from the long winter, may discard 2 grain to gain 1 wood") is harder to parse and less precise. Save the world voice for headers, callouts, and intros.

## Choosing the in-world voice

Your game has a voice — find it. A few prompts:

- Who is the *narrator* of the rulebook? Are they someone in the world, or someone outside it?
- What does the world *care about*? A world about scarcity should sound hungry. A world about ambition should sound covetous. A world about wonder should sound curious.
- What are the world's *forbidden words*? (A medieval-set rulebook shouldn't say "leverage." A spacefaring one shouldn't say "thee.")

Then apply the voice consistently — to the intro, to flavor headers, to faction descriptions, to the back-of-box copy. **Not** to rule text.

## Worked example

**Bad** (mixed voice):

> When it is the active player's turn, that player, drawing upon the wisdom of their ancestors, may take 2 actions, though they should choose carefully, for the gods are watching.

**Better** (separated voice):

> Procedural: On your turn, you take 2 actions.
> 
> Header (in-world): *The gods are watching.*
> 
> Or in a callout: "*The gods are watching.* — what your character believes about every action this game."

## Voice for the introduction

The intro page is your one chance to set tone before rules begin. Two paragraphs, the world's voice, *no rules.*

**Example (cozy game):**

> The forest has woken from a long winter. New shoots are pushing up through last year's leaves; rivers run again; the birds are coming back. You and the others are gardeners — settling into a quiet corner of the woods, planting what you've brought, watching what grows.
>
> Over six seasons, you'll plant, harvest, and arrange your garden. The most balanced and beautiful garden at the end of fall is the one that endures. *(For how to play, see Game Overview, page 4.)*

**Example (dark fantasy):**

> Three years after the last Lord fell, the keep is still ours by inheritance and by stubbornness. The roads no longer carry merchants — they carry refugees, brigands, and worse. Each of you is the head of a family with stake in this land. None of you trust the others. None of you can survive alone.
>
> Over twelve seasons, you'll bargain, raid, and rebuild. The first family to claim the keep — and hold it — will write the next chapter of this country's history. *(For how to play, see Game Overview, page 4.)*

The intro voice is how the player decides whether they're in the right book.

## Voice for flavor text on cards

Cards are read every turn. Their flavor is the highest-volume narrative surface in the game.

Principles:

- **Keep it short.** One line, two at most. The card's mechanic comes first; flavor comes second.
- **Speak in-world.** A first-person quote, an in-world fragment, an in-world detail. Avoid rules-explanatory flavor text ("This card is great when…").
- **Earn the space.** If the card has 30 words of mechanical text, it doesn't need 20 words of flavor. The flavor can be a single line.
- **Cut anything generic.** "Lightning strikes" is wallpaper. "The first lightning of the year woke the children" is in your world.

## Voice for designer notes

Designer notes go at the very end. This is where you are *yourself*, talking to the player about the game.

What designer notes should do:

- Thank playtesters by name.
- Mention the inspirations that shaped the design.
- Acknowledge the parts that took the longest, or the rule you almost cut, or the test that changed everything.
- Recommend what to play after you've enjoyed this one.

What they should *not* do:

- Explain rules.
- Apologize for design choices.
- Sell future expansions hard.

A good designer note is a short letter. Two paragraphs. Warm, specific, signed.

## Voice consistency check

A rulebook with consistent voice has:

- The intro voice matching the back-of-box copy.
- The flavor text matching the intro voice.
- The card names matching the flavor text.
- The faction descriptions matching the card names.
- The procedural rules in their own clean voice, not contaminated by the world voice.

If the user has a draft, run a spot check: pull one paragraph from the intro, one card name, one faction description, and one piece of flavor text. Do they sound like the same world? If not, name where they diverge.
