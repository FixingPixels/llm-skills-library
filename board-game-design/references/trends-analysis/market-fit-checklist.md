# Market Fit Checklist

Run this when a designer wants a structured read on whether their game has a market, where it lives, and what it's competing with.

## Step 1: Name the comparison set (5-10 games)

Without 5+ comparison games, no market analysis is reliable. If the designer can't name them:

- They haven't played enough in their target space.
- The space may be more crowded than they realize.
- They're at risk of designing something that already exists, badly.

Push for: 3 close comparisons (same genre, similar weight, similar audience), 2-3 adjacent (same audience, different mechanic, or vice versa), 1-2 aspirational (the best games in the space, even if at higher production scale).

## Step 2: Position on the four axes

For each comparable, place the game on:

- **Weight** (light → heavy)
- **Length** (under 30 min → 90+ min)
- **Player count** sweet spot
- **Conflict level** (cozy/cooperative → direct attack)

Where does the user's design sit relative to comparables? If it sits *exactly* where one of them does, the design needs a sharper differentiator. If it sits in a gap, that's interesting — investigate why no one is in that gap (often there's a reason).

## Step 3: Differentiator audit

The user should be able to answer:

- **What's the one thing this does that no comparable does?**
- **Why hasn't someone done it already?** (Honest answer: it's risky, it's hard, the audience is small, the mechanic is fragile, or… it's been done and you don't know.)
- **Is the differentiator the *hook* or the *fine print*?** A differentiator on the back of the box (visible to a buyer) is far stronger than one buried in rule 12.

If the only differentiator is "better art" or "better balance," that's not a market position. The market doesn't reward incremental improvements unless distribution is solved.

## Step 4: Audience size sanity check

For the target audience, rough estimates:

- **Mass-market party / family** — millions of potential buyers, but distribution is the bottleneck (retail relationships, not BGG).
- **Hobby midweight / gateway** — hundreds of thousands of potential buyers.
- **Heavy euro / strategy** — tens of thousands of dedicated buyers.
- **Heavy ameritrash / dungeon crawl** — tens of thousands, often via Kickstarter.
- **Wargames, 18xx** — single-digit thousands, deeply committed.
- **Print-and-play / pure niche** — hundreds, communities.

The user's expectations should match the audience size. A wargame won't sell 50,000 copies. A party game can. Mismatched expectations kill projects.

## Step 5: Production-cost / price-point sanity check

Match audience to budget:

- A heavy euro audience tolerates $60-$100 game prices but expects high-quality components.
- A family audience expects $30-$50 prices and is sensitive to overproduction.
- A Kickstarter dungeon crawl audience tolerates $100-$200+ but expects miniatures.
- A roll-and-write audience expects $15-$30 and a small footprint.

Solo hobbyists often *under*-estimate the cost of producing the game they imagine. Even self-published, prototypes cost real money. If the user's design has 100+ unique cards, custom dice, custom miniatures, and a campaign book, they're not making a $30 game.

## Step 6: Distribution path

How will the game reach players?

- **Self-publish via Kickstarter / Gamefound** — audience-building required (12+ months), platform fees ~10%, production logistics complex.
- **Self-publish print-and-play** — minimal audience, minimal cost, free distribution via BGG and Itch.
- **License to a publisher** — long timelines (12-36 months from signing to release), small royalty (~5-10%), but no production risk.
- **Self-publish to retail** — usually requires a distributor relationship; non-trivial.
- **Digital adaptation first** (Tabletopia, Tabletop Simulator, Steam) — different model, different audience.

The right distribution path depends on the user's goals. Hobby designers often default to "Kickstarter" without understanding the audience-building burden. License-to-publisher is overlooked but often the best fit for a hobbyist with a strong design and no audience.

## Step 7: Honest verdict

After steps 1-6, ask:

- **Is the audience real?** Specific. "Co-op gamers" isn't real; "co-op gamers who want a 30-min experience that's harder than *Forbidden Island*" might be.
- **Is the differentiator strong enough to break in?** Or is the user designing a "me-too" that needs marketing budget to win?
- **Is the production cost / price match achievable?** If not, redesign or accept a smaller audience.
- **Is the distribution path realistic for the user's situation?**

If 3+ of these are no, the user has work to do — not necessarily on the design, but on the framing, the audience definition, or the production approach.

## Red flags to surface honestly

- Designer can't name comparables.
- Designer thinks "no one has made this."
- Designer's pitch starts with the components or art, not the experience.
- Designer expects a Kickstarter to "fund itself" without an audience.
- Designer is designing for "everyone."
- Designer hasn't played 10+ games in the target space.
- Designer's target audience is themselves and their two friends.

These aren't disqualifying — they're just signals that more market work is needed before production work.
