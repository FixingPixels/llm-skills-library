# Theme as Design

Theme that *works* shows up in the rules, the components, the names, and the moments at the table. Theme that doesn't work lives in the rulebook intro and nowhere else.

## The "show me" test

For every narrative element, ask: where does this **show up at the table**?

- A faction's history → does it manifest in their power, their name, their starting position, their starting hand? If not, players will never know it.
- A world's geography → does it shape the board, the routes, the resources? If not, it's flavor.
- A character's personality → does it appear in *how they play differently*? If not, the character is a portrait, not a role.

If the user describes lore that lives only in the rulebook, gently ask: what could you *cut from the rulebook* and *add to the game* that would tell the same story?

## Worked examples

### Working integration: *Root* (Cole Wehrle)

The world is a forest where small animals fight for control. Each faction plays *radically* differently — not "different stats" but different verb sets:

- The Marquise builds and protects industry; her fiction is a colonial occupation, and her play *feels* like managing a sprawling logistical empire.
- The Eyrie are bound to a Decree they can't break; their fiction is a brittle aristocracy, and their play *feels* like watching a political body collapse under its own rules.
- The Vagabond is alone; his fiction is a wanderer with no allegiances, and his play *feels* like solo problem-solving in a multiplayer game.

The asymmetry isn't decoration — it *is* the politics of the world. Cut any one faction's mechanics and you don't have a less-balanced game; you have a different world.

### Working integration: *This War of Mine: The Board Game*

The fiction is civilians surviving a siege. The mechanics are scarcity, exhaustion, sickness, sleep. The board is a damaged building. Every system makes the player feel the world: searching at night is dangerous (real game-cost), trading with hostile NPCs has unpredictable outcomes (real risk), and characters psychologically break (real penalty).

The lesson: when the fiction is "civilians in war," the mechanics are *about* powerlessness, scarcity, moral compromise. Compare to a war game where you "play civilians" but the mechanics are still resource optimization — that's wallpaper.

### Failed integration: many fantasy euro re-skins

Take a clean economic engine — convert wood to coins, coins to victory points — and skin it as "elven trade routes" or "merchant guilds of Atlantis." The math is the same; only the box art changes. Players notice. The game lives or dies on the engine, and the theme adds nothing to the experience of playing.

The lesson: if you can swap the theme without rewriting any rules, the theme isn't doing work. Either commit to the theme by changing the design, or drop the theme.

## Pulling theme through specific design surfaces

A practical checklist for surfaces a designer can integrate theme into:

- **Card names** — should sound like the world, not like the mechanic. "Lightning Strike" is generic; "Last Light Before the Storm" is specific.
- **Resource names** — generic resources (wood, coin, mana) are signals of weak theme integration. Custom resources (grain rations, blood debt, signal flares, faith) tie the world to the mechanic.
- **Action verbs** — what the player physically does should sound like what their character does. "Take 2 actions" is mechanical; "Send a runner; Forge a deal" is in-world.
- **Component shapes** — meeples shaped like settlers, soldiers, scientists tell more story than generic cubes. Within budget, theme can win.
- **Game phases** — phase names matter. "Phase 1: Setup" is mechanical; "Phase 1: The First Frost" is fictional.
- **Turn ends** — what triggers the end of the game? "After 8 rounds" is mechanical; "When the last well runs dry" is thematic.
- **Scoring categories** — score categories named in-world (Reputation, Honor, Knowledge, Heir's Inheritance) carry theme; score categories named mechanically (Set A, Set B, Bonus) don't.

## When mechanics fight theme

If a mechanic and the theme contradict, fix one of them. Both must change or both must stay and accept the tension.

Common conflicts:

- **"Diplomatic" theme + zero-sum scoring.** If the only way to win is to crush opponents, "diplomacy" is wallpaper.
- **"Cooperative" theme + take-that mechanics.** Pick. Hybrid is hard to land.
- **"Survival" theme + abundant resources.** If players never feel scarcity, the survival theme is decorative.
- **"Heroic" theme + no agency in the climax.** If the final turn is a die roll, the heroism is hollow.

When you spot one of these, name it. The user can either change the mechanic, change the theme, or commit to the tension as design (some games — *Battlestar Galactica*, for example — *use* the contradiction).

## Voice and tone of the world

The world has a voice. Find it early; apply it consistently.

Questions to elicit voice:

- If the world wrote you a letter, what would the letter sound like? Formal? Bitter? Hopeful? Hungry?
- Who is the *narrator* of the rulebook? Neutral, in-world, ironic, mythic?
- What does the world *not* say? (A world that never mentions money is different from one that never mentions love.)

The voice should appear in: card names, flavor text, faction descriptions, rulebook framing, even action prompts. A consistent voice is half of a strong theme.
