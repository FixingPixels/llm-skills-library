# Faction Design Prompts

When a designer is building asymmetric factions, characters, or playable roles. Push them past "the fast one and the strong one."

## The five-question faction sheet

For each faction, the designer should be able to answer all five. If they can't, the faction isn't real yet.

1. **Who are they?** One paragraph. Their history, their stake in the world, their voice. Not their power.
2. **What do they want?** Their goal, distinct from other factions'. Different goals are stronger than different paths to the same goal.
3. **What can they do that no one else can?** The mechanical signature.
4. **What can't they do that everyone else can?** The mechanical constraint. As identity-defining as the power.
5. **What does their player feel?** The experience of playing this faction. Different *texture*, not just different *strategy*.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- **The stat-line faction.** "Faction A is fast but weak; Faction B is slow but strong." That's a tuning dial, not a faction. Push for different *verbs*, not different *numbers*.
- **The genre-cliché faction.** "The dwarves dig; the elves shoot; the orcs smash." Allowed, but only if the designer has done specific work to make *their* dwarves/elves/orcs different from every other game's. Otherwise it's wallpaper.
- **The optimization-trap faction.** A faction whose power is so strong or so weak that it dominates strategy. Either it's an autopick or a never-pick. Both kill asymmetry.
- **The mirror faction.** Two factions that play almost identically with cosmetic differences. Cut one.
- **The committee-designed faction.** A faction with five abilities, none of which feel essential. Cut to one or two signature abilities and let the rest be flavor.

## Goal-asymmetry vs. capability-asymmetry

Most asymmetric games do **capability asymmetry** — same victory condition, different ways to pursue it. *Root* does **goal asymmetry** — each faction wins by different rules entirely. Goal asymmetry is harder but produces stronger faction identity.

Help the designer name which they're doing, and consider whether they should push further toward goal asymmetry. Even a single faction with a different victory condition (the traitor in *Battlestar Galactica*, the Vagabond in *Root*) lifts the whole game.

## Faction voice

Each faction should *sound* different. Even subtle differences read at the table.

Tools for differentiating voice:

- **Naming conventions.** Faction A's units are named for animals; Faction B's for weather. Card titles, unit names, resources — pull through a faction-specific lens.
- **Color palette.** Beyond the player color: do their cards have a different visual signature? Their illustrations a different mood?
- **Iconography.** Do their icons feel like they came from the same culture as their voice?
- **Mechanic vocabulary.** Faction A "raids"; Faction B "harvests"; Faction C "negotiates." Even if the mechanic is similar, the word makes the faction.

## Testing faction balance

Two heuristics, one practical, one cultural:

- **Practical**: Track win rate across many games. After ~20 games, if one faction is below 30% or above 60%, it likely needs tuning. (Sample sizes are small for hobbyists — combine personal data with online playtest reports.)
- **Cultural**: Ask players which faction they'd play *next*. If one faction is always the second pick, it's underpowered. If one is always the first, it's either overpowered or just more attractive (which is fine if win rates are even).

Asymmetry doesn't have to be perfectly balanced. A 45/55 split is fine if both factions are *fun*. The fix for an underloved faction is often to make it more *interesting*, not more *strong*.

## When asymmetry isn't the right answer

Some games are stronger symmetric. Reasons to *not* make a game asymmetric:

- The fun is in the puzzle, not the role. (Pure puzzles like *The Crew* don't need asymmetric players.)
- The game is short. (Asymmetry takes time to learn; short games punish learning.)
- The audience is casual. (Asymmetry adds explanation overhead. Family games rarely benefit.)
- The designer can't yet make one faction feel right. (One unrealized faction is worse than a clean symmetric design.)

If a designer wants asymmetry "because that's what good games have," push back. Asymmetry is a tool. The question is what experience it produces — not whether to have it.
