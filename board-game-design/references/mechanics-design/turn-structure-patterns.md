# Turn Structure Patterns

Quick reference of common turn structures and what they're good for. Use to help a designer choose a structure deliberately rather than defaulting to "we go in a circle."

## Sequential turns (round-the-table)

The default. Player takes a full turn, then next player.

- **Good for**: Heavy strategy where each turn deserves attention; games where blocking and reacting matter; trick-taking and similar where order is the rule.
- **Bad for**: Long turns (downtime explodes); 5+ players (waiting between turns hurts).
- **Tightening tools**: Shorter turns, simultaneous sub-phases, action-points cap.

## Action points / action budget

Each turn the active player has N actions or N points to spend among options.

- **Good for**: Games where flexibility is part of the experience (Pandemic, Mage Knight).
- **Bad for**: AP-prone players (combinatorial explosion).
- **Tightening tools**: Small action budgets (3-4 max); each action type listed simply; cost variation forces tradeoffs.

## Role / role-selection

Each turn a player picks a role; the role's special power applies; everyone takes the role's action.

- **Good for**: Player interaction with no direct conflict; games where what your opponents are doing matters as much as your own choice (Puerto Rico, Race for the Galaxy, Citadels).
- **Bad for**: Solo or two-player without modification; designs where the role pool needs to be small but the game is long.
- **Tightening tools**: Role refresh between rounds; bonus for picking unpicked roles; role costs.

## Worker placement

Players take turns placing workers on action spaces, blocking each other.

- **Good for**: Tension over scarce actions; visible competition; medium-weight strategy (Agricola, Caverna, Lords of Waterdeep).
- **Bad for**: Designs with abundant actions (no scarcity = no tension); short games (setup + recall costs time).
- **Tightening tools**: Action spaces with diminishing returns; spaces that open or close based on game state; worker upgrades that change placement priority.

## Simultaneous action

All players act at the same time, often by selecting cards or programming moves.

- **Good for**: Eliminating downtime; bluffing and mind-reading (7 Wonders, RoboRally); high-player-count games.
- **Bad for**: Heavy strategy where each turn needs to digest the previous one; designs that need turn-by-turn information cascades.
- **Tightening tools**: Phased reveal; resolution priority rules; tiebreakers.

## Drafting (within turns or between)

Players take from a shared pool, passing the rest.

- **Good for**: Decision-density (every pick matters); player interaction without conflict; replayability through different drafts (7 Wonders, Sushi Go).
- **Bad for**: Designs where the optimal pick is always obvious; games where the pool can't be visually scanned quickly.
- **Tightening tools**: Pack size tuned to player count; rare cards seeded; pick-and-pass-and-burn patterns.

## Programmed / pre-committed

Players plan multiple turns in advance, then execute.

- **Good for**: Comedic chaos when plans collide (RoboRally); puzzle-solving under uncertainty.
- **Bad for**: Designs where the comedy/chaos isn't part of the experience.
- **Tightening tools**: Limit programming horizon (3-5 actions); allow partial reveals; allow last-minute changes at a cost.

## Real-time

No turns; play continues until a trigger.

- **Good for**: Tension, party games, dexterity; eliminating downtime entirely (Spaceteam, Magic Maze, Captain Sonar).
- **Bad for**: Heavy strategy; players who want to think; accessibility (timing-impaired players struggle).
- **Tightening tools**: Pause mechanics; phase-based real-time alternating with planning.

## Hybrid / phased

Combines two structures across phases of the game.

- **Good for**: Modeling phases of fiction (e.g., war games with planning phase + simultaneous combat); managing pacing across acts.
- **Bad for**: Adding complexity for its own sake — make sure the phases each *do* something different.
- **Tightening tools**: Make phase transitions visible and dramatic; let phases differ in pacing (long planning, fast execution).

## Choosing a structure

Don't pick a structure first and design into it. Pick a structure because:

1. It produces the *kind of decision* the game is about.
2. It produces the *pace* the experience demands.
3. It scales to the *player count* you're targeting.
4. It manages downtime and AP for *your audience*.

If two structures fit, prototype both. The designer's gut is often right but sometimes lazy.
